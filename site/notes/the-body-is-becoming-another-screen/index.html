The Body Is Becoming Another Screen

We spent centuries trying to escape the machine. Now we're wearing it on our wrists.

There was a time when your body was just your body.

You got tired, you slept. You got hungry, you ate. You felt like shit, you rested. You felt strong, you trained. Nobody needed an app to tell them they'd slept badly. Their own fucking brain handled that.

Now we've strapped computers to our wrists and convinced ourselves that freedom means receiving more detailed notifications about our own biological decline.

Sleep score. Readiness score. HRV. Resting heart rate. Calories burned. Steps. Recovery. Stress. Strain. Glucose. Temperature.

Every morning, millions of people wake up and ask a piece of silicon how they're supposed to feel.

That's progress, apparently.

Some of this technology is genuinely useful. Wearables can reveal patterns you'd never notice yourself. Glucose monitors can teach people about metabolism. Heart-rate data can help track training load. Sleep tracking can expose habits that were previously invisible.

The problem starts when measurement quietly becomes authority.

You stop listening to your body and start negotiating with the dashboard.

Your sleep score is 62, therefore today is a bad day. Your HRV dropped, therefore you're stressed. Your recovery score says 41, therefore you shouldn't train.

The machine has become the priest.

You bring it your biological sins every morning and wait for judgement.

We did this with work. Then money. Then education. Then social media. Now we're doing it with our fucking nervous systems.

Everything must be quantified. Everything must improve. Everything must become an optimisation problem.

You don't simply sleep anymore. You optimise sleep.

You don't exercise. You optimise performance.

You don't eat. You optimise nutrition.

You don't live. You optimise your biomarkers.

At some point, the pursuit of health becomes another form of anxiety.

The biohacking world understands this better than almost anyone. There's always another intervention. Another supplement. Another protocol. Another molecule. Another wearable. Another morning routine. Another obscure longevity researcher whose podcast appearance somehow convinced you that your current biological existence is an unacceptable draft version of yourself.

The industry sells a beautiful fantasy:

You are a machine.

Therefore, if you can identify enough variables, measure enough outputs and manipulate enough inputs, you can eventually engineer yourself into something better.

There's truth buried inside that idea. Human beings are adaptable. Training matters. Nutrition matters. Sleep matters. Environment matters. Medicine matters. Technology can extend what we're capable of doing.

But there's a dangerous philosophical shift hiding underneath the optimisation obsession.

If your body is a machine, when are you finally allowed to stop upgrading it?

At 25? 35? 50? Never?

What happens when your resting heart rate is excellent, your bloodwork looks fantastic, your VO₂ max is impressive, your body fat is low, your sleep tracker is delighted...

...and you're still miserable?

That's the part nobody wants to put on the product page.

You can optimise the organism while completely fucking up the life.

You can become extraordinarily good at maintaining a body you don't actually enjoy inhabiting.

And perhaps that's the next stage of the digital age.

Not machines replacing humans.

Something stranger.

Humans voluntarily turning themselves into machines.

We measure ourselves. Score ourselves. Track ourselves. Correct ourselves. Punish ourselves. Upgrade ourselves.

Then wonder why we feel so disconnected from the thing we're supposedly trying to improve.

Maybe the future of biohacking isn't learning how to control every biological variable.

Maybe it's learning which variables don't need controlling.

Sometimes you're tired because you're tired. Sometimes you eat because something tastes fucking good. Sometimes you train badly. Sometimes you sleep terribly. Sometimes you do absolutely nothing productive for an entire afternoon.

And none of that necessarily means you've failed.

A human being isn't a dashboard.

You're not supposed to achieve 100% uptime.

You're supposed to fucking live.
